Venice is not a city to "do": it is a city to cross in a certain order, at certain hours. The problem is not the city, it is the moment you arrive. This guide puts together what the official pages of the City and of AVM say (access fee, vaporetto, rules of conduct) with what tourism sites say about hours, quieter districts and bacari, flagging every time where sources disagree. Most of the sources are in Italian, so the names of pages and terms are given as they appear.

> **In short.** No, there is no fee to enter Venice today: the 2026 access-fee trial ended on 26 July and the City's official site says nothing is due. It returns in 2027 on fixed days between 26 March and 15 August, 8:30 to 16:30, with amounts still to be announced. To beat the crowds go early in the morning or after sunset, spend the middle of the day in Cannaregio and Castello, and eat at the counter in a bacaro rather than at the tables around San Marco. A 75-minute vaporetto ticket costs 9.50 € on the official AVM page; between November and April also check the tide forecast. Prices and days change, so check the official pages.

## Do I have to pay to enter Venice?

The access fee is the most frequent question, and it is also where the sources contradict each other. The City of Venice's information page, updated on 13 March 2026, still describes the 2026 calendar: from 3 April to 26 July, for 60 non-consecutive days. The official fee site (cda.ve.it), on the other hand, states at the top that the 2026 trial has ended and that from 27 July 2026 the fee does not apply: you can visit Venice without any payment or request for exemption. This second statement is the most recent, and it matches today's date: no fee to pay.

For the future, the same site lists the 2027 days: March 26, 27, 28, 29; April 2, 3, 4, 9, 10, 11, 16, 17, 18 and 23 to 30; May 1, 2, 7, 8, 9, 14, 15, 16, 21, 22, 23 and 28 to 31; June 1 to 6, 11, 12, 13, 18, 19, 20, 25, 26, 27; July 2, 3, 4, 9, 10, 11, 16, 17, 18, 23, 24, 25, 30, 31; August 1, 6, 7, 8, 13, 14, 15. The time band is 8:30 to 16:30. Procedures and amount for 2027 are not published: the site says they will be announced by 31 December and that the 2027 FAQ are not yet available. From the City's page, which refers to 2026, the fee applied to people over 14 visiting the old city, and those excluded or exempt included residents of the municipality of Venice, workers and students, people staying overnight in accommodation within the municipality (who must still register on the portal) and residents of the Veneto region. The penalty for not paying was 25 to 150 euros (plus the fee). These are 2026 rules: check them again before a trip in 2027.

## How do you avoid the crowds in practice?

The simplest strategy is also the oldest one: change the hour. A local guide read for this article lists early morning as the quietest time, when canals and lanes are still empty, and the evening after sunset, when many day visitors have left; it adds that the low season, from November to February excluding holiday periods, is a good time for a calmer Venice. The same logic appears in the City of Venice's guidance, which among its 12 good practices asks visitors to "plan the trip on days of lower traffic" and to stay more than one day to get to know the lesser-known places too.

The second lever is the district. Local sites point to Cannaregio and Castello as the most authentic sestieri: in Cannaregio the church of Madonna dell'Orto (where Tintoretto's tomb is) and the Misericordia canal, in Castello Via Garibaldi, the gardens and the Arsenale. Among lesser-known places the same guide cites the cloisters of the Carmelitani Scalzi and of San Francesco della Vigna. Among the official Venezia Unica itineraries there is one on the Giudecca and San Giorgio Maggiore that ends with the view from the San Giorgio bell tower, 75 metres tall, and one on Cannaregio. Keep in mind that the more commercial tourism sites also carry paid offers: I used only the descriptive information, not the purchase suggestions.

## Vaporetto: which ticket do you really need?

On the official AVM site the main prices are these: ordinary 75-minute ticket on the single network 9.50 €, 24 hours 25 €, 48 hours 35 €, 72 hours 45 €, 7 days 65 €. The time ticket is valid on all land and water vehicles and allows unlimited trips within the time band that starts at first validation; connections to Marco Polo airport are not included. The ticket must be validated as soon as you board, by touching it to the validator, and each change of vehicle needs a new validation. The page also lists a "Rolling Venice - Youth" offer with a 72-hour ticket at 27 €; on the Venezia Unica site the same combination (ACTV 72 hours + Rolling Venice) appears instead as "from 33 €". Two official pages, two figures: I did not read the conditions, so check the price when you buy.

A calculation that helps: two 75-minute tickets cost 19 €, less than the 24-hour ticket (25 €); three tickets cost 28.50 €, more than the 24-hour one. So the 24-hour ticket only pays if you expect at least three trips at different times of the day. These are sums on today's indicative prices and depend on how you use the lines; in Venice many stretches are done on foot anyway.

## How should you behave?

The City of Venice runs a campaign, "Enjoy Respect Venezia", with 12 concrete gestures. Some apply to everyone: walk on the right and in single file in narrow lanes, do not stop on bridges, do not lead bicycles even by hand; do not use church steps, wells, quays and monuments as picnic areas (for eating there are the public gardens); around Piazza San Marco you may not stop outside the designated spaces to eat or drink; camping or bivouacking, walking about shirtless, diving or swimming are not allowed; do not feed the pigeons and do not leave padlocks. These are the rules that make the difference between a good day and a fine, and between being a guest and being a weight on the city.

## Where to eat for real: the bacaro

The bacaro is Venice's typical tavern: small, rustic, often wooden, with the cicchetti displayed in a case near the counter. According to the Visit Venezia site (which notes commercial partnerships in its text) the most common cicchetti are sarde in saor (sweet-and-sour sardines), baccalà mantecato (whipped salt cod), meatballs, mozzarelle in carrozza, fried fish and tramezzini, and they go with an ombra of wine or a spritz. The round from bacaro to bacaro is done at lunchtime or at aperitivo time; the same source notes that many tourists take a tray of cicchetti as a full meal, and that this misses the point of the ritual, which is a roaming tasting.

How much does it cost? The site gives 0.60 to 2 € for a glass of wine and 1 to 3 € for a cicchetto. The page is undated, so treat it as an order of magnitude, not a price list. For areas, it cites Cannaregio (along the Fondamenta degli Ormesini), Santa Croce, Dorsoduro (Campo Santa Margherita) and Castello, described as the least touristy sestiere and therefore rich in bacari frequented by locals. I am not giving you the name of a specific place: I have not verified it on an official page and bacari change management and hours. The practical criterion is different: choose where you see people chatting at the counter, not where a waiter hands you a menu in the street.

## Rialto market: when to go

At Rialto you find the historic market, with the fish market and the Erbaria area. Two Venetian accommodations that describe the market on their sites give hours that are nearly identical but not the same: the fish market Tuesday to Saturday (7:30 to 12:00) for one, Tuesday to Friday (7:30 to 12:00) for the other; fruit and vegetables Monday to Saturday until 13:30 for both. A third source read in the November review, a Venice guide site that discloses commercial partners, gives the fish market as Tuesday to Saturday (7:00 to 14:00 on its card) and fruit and vegetables Monday to Saturday, 7:00 to 13:30: with it, three sites out of four give Saturday, but the fish-market hours range from 7:30-12:00 to 7:00-14:00. Saturday remains the day to verify: I found no City page resolving the discrepancy. One of the two sites also recalls that the fish market's Loggia dates from 1907, the work of Domenico Rupolo, with different capitals inspired by fishing, and that the island of Sant'Erasmo is traditionally tied to the produce sold here. If you want to see the market at its best, go in the first part of the morning: it is the moment when daily life is most visible. Later, the stalls empty out.

## What if there is acqua alta?

If you go between November and April, the tide matters as much as the weather. The City's page says that from 15 September to 30 April the "Piano di Viabilità Pedonale", the raised walkways, is activated based on Tide Centre forecasts; the marked routes keep you dry up to a tide of +120 cm, and above that some may be unusable. The Tide Centre publishes a scale of levels: normal tide between -50 and +80 cm, "sustained" between +80 and +110, "very sustained" between +110 and +140, exceptional between +140 and +200. The same page shows the forecast for the next few days with the times of highs and lows, and lists webcams, warning sirens and an FAQ among its services.

In practice: look at the forecast the evening before and again in the morning, and if a high peak is announced, move your walk to the low-tide hours, which the forecast gives with times. The walkway atlases are separate PDFs by district (San Marco, Castello, Cannaregio, San Polo, Santa Croce, Dorsoduro, Giudecca, Murano, Burano); the overview map on the page, however, dates from 2019-20, so use it as orientation, not as proof that a given walkway is in place that day. One thing no source I read says is where to rent or buy boots, so I will not say. ## How do I get from the airport, and what does my ticket not cover?

This is the most common mistake on arrival: assuming a vaporetto ticket also covers the airport. On the AVM site, connections to Marco Polo airport are not included in the single ticket or in the time-based ones. The Venezia Unica site lists ACTV tickets (from 9.50 € for 75 minutes, from 25 € for the day pass) separately from Alilaguna, the airport boat service: Alilaguna time-limited tickets, covering the blue, orange and red lines with the Marco Polo transfer included, start from 40 €. The People Mover, the automated link between Piazzale Roma and Tronchetto, costs from 1.50 € for a 75-minute ride. For exact times and lines use the operators' pages: I have not quoted them because they change.

## The mistakes visitors make most often

- **Thinking the access fee is still due.** The City's information page still describes the 2026 calendar; the official site says that from 27 July 2026 it does not apply. The 2027 days are already listed, but amounts and procedures are not.
- **Not validating at every change of vehicle.** AVM says validation is compulsory as soon as you board and must be repeated each time you change.
- **Buying the 24-hour pass for two rides.** Two 75-minute tickets cost 19 €; the day pass costs 25 €.
- **Going to Rialto on a Monday for the fish.** According to the sources the fish market is closed on Monday and Sunday; on Monday only the fruit and vegetable part is open.
- **Arriving late at the market.** One source suggests going before 9 am, when the stalls are still a workplace; many small stalls prefer cash, and the stallholder picks the fish, not the customer.
- **Looking for a market only at Rialto.** For where Venetians shop, listings point to the Via Garibaldi market in Castello (Monday to Saturday, roughly 7:00 to 14:00) and the Rio Terà San Leonardo market in Cannaregio (Monday to Saturday, 7:30 to 13:00): these are "guide" hours from a tourism site, so check them on the spot.

## What to leave out

- Piazza San Marco at peak hour: look at it at the start or end of the day and give the middle of the day to something else.
- The seated meal at the most photographed spots: move two or three lanes away and look for the counter with people standing.
- The idea of doing everything in one day: Venice rewards those who stay longer and do not chase lists.
